\textbf{1.} What does the following signal processing concepts mean?
Be clear, concise, and relevant.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*),leftmargin=15pt,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
\item Convolution\hfill(1p)
\item Transfer function\hfill(1p)
\item Impulse response\hfill(1p)

\end{enumerate}

\SignalSolution{1}{a}{%
\textit{Graded formula retained; definition checked against the course lectures.}
\stepheading{Convolution}
For an LTI system, convolve the input with the impulse response to find the output:
\[
\begin{aligned}
y[n]&=(x*h)[n]\\
&=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}x[k]h[n-k].
\end{aligned}
\]
\textbf{English clarification:} Reverse one signal, shift it, multiply the overlapping
values, then sum.
In the frequency domain it becomes multiplication:
\[
Y(f)=X(f)H(f).
\]
\noteheading{Final answer}
Convolution combines two signals by reversing and shifting one, multiplying overlapping values,
and summing them (or integrating in continuous time).
\noteheading{Remember}
Time domain: convolve. Frequency domain: multiply.
}

\SignalSolution{1}{b}{%
\textit{Your input-output relation, checked against the course definition.}
\stepheading{Transfer function}
A transfer function describes an LTI system's input-output relation in the transform domain.
Starting from rest:
\[
\begin{aligned}
H(s)&=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}\quad\text{(continuous time)},\\
H(z)&=\frac{Y(z)}{X(z)}\quad\text{(discrete time)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
It is the Laplace or Z-transform of the impulse response.
\par
\textbf{English clarification:} The January graded definition describes a Fourier
transform. Here we need the system's transformed output/input ratio.
\noteheading{Final answer}
The transfer function is the ratio of transformed output to transformed input,
with zero initial conditions.
\noteheading{Remember}
$Y=HX$: input multiplied by the system gives the output.
}

\SignalSolution{1}{c}{%
\textit{Cross-year graded reference: 2024-10, 1/1 point.}\par
\stepheading{Impulse response}
The impulse response is a system's response, or output, to an impulse input.
\par
\textbf{English clarification:} Starting from rest, a unit impulse at the input
gives the impulse response at the output:
\[
\begin{aligned}
x[n]&=\delta[n],\\
y[n]&=h[n].
\end{aligned}
\]
\noteheading{Final answer}
The impulse response $h[n]$ is the output produced by a unit impulse input.
\noteheading{Remember}
Unit impulse in; impulse response out.
}

\SignalSolution{1}{d}{%
\textit{Cross-year graded reference: 2024-10, 1/1 point.}\par
\stepheading{Sampling}
Turn a continuous signal into a discrete signal by taking sample points at a
predetermined sampling frequency:
\[
S(t)\longrightarrow S(n).
\]
\noteheading{Final answer}
Sampling converts a continuous-time signal into a discrete-time signal by
taking measurements at regular time intervals.
\noteheading{Remember}
Sampling makes the time axis discrete.
}


\SignalSolution{1}{e}{%
\textit{Your 2026-01 handwritten folding example; definition checked against Lecture 2.}
\stepheading{Aliasing}
When the sampling rate is too low, different continuous-time frequencies become
indistinguishable in the samples. A higher frequency can appear as a lower frequency.

Example from your notes: a $25\,\mathrm{Hz}$ component sampled at $30\,\mathrm{Hz}$ folds to
\[
f_{\mathrm{alias}}=|25-30|=5\,\mathrm{Hz}.
\]
\noteheading{Final answer}
Aliasing is frequency confusion caused by overlapping spectral copies during
sampling, so the original signal cannot be recovered uniquely.
\noteheading{Remember}
Sampling too slowly can make a high frequency look lower.
}

\stepheading{2. Look for feedback}

\stepheading{3. Decide FIR or IIR}

\textbf{IIR:} infinite impulse response.\par

\SignalSolution{2}{b}{%
\textit{Your method: input group + feedback group, then combine.}\par
\noteheading{Read the blocks}
Each $T$ adds one sample of delay.
Multiply each branch by its gain, then add the terms entering the sums.

\stepheading{1. Input group}
Follow the branches starting at $x(n)$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{No delay:}\quad &1\cdot x(n)=x(n),\\
\text{One delay:}\quad &4x(n-1),\\
\text{Three delays:}\quad &2x(n-3).
\end{aligned}
\]
There is no branch into a sum after the second $T$, so there is no $x(n-2)$ term.
Add the input terms:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Input group}&=x(n)+4x(n-1)\\
                &\quad+2x(n-3).
\end{aligned}
\]

\stepheading{2. Feedback group}
Now start at $y(n)$ and follow the return branches.
Use this exam's gains, $+0.4$ and $-0.2$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{One delay:}\quad &+0.4y(n-1),\\
\text{Three delays:}\quad &-0.2y(n-3).
\end{aligned}
\]
The second delay has no branch into the sum, so there is no $y(n-2)$ term.
Add the feedback terms:
\[
\text{Feedback group}=0.4y(n-1)-0.2y(n-3).
\]

\stepheading{3. Combine at the output}
The final $+$ adds your two groups:
\[
y(n)=\text{Input group}+\text{Feedback group}.
\]
Substitute the grouped terms:
\[
\begin{aligned}
y(n)&=x(n)+4x(n-1)\\
    &\quad+2x(n-3)\\
    &\quad+0.4y(n-1)-0.2y(n-3).
\end{aligned}
\]

\stepheading{4. Collect the output terms}
Move the feedback terms to the left.
Their signs change.

\noteheading{Final answer}
\[
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
&y(n)-0.4y(n-1)+0.2y(n-3)\\
&\quad=x(n)+4x(n-1)+2x(n-3).
\end{aligned}}
\]

\noteheading{Remember}
Count delays to each tapped branch, keep the gain signs, then combine the groups.
}

\SignalSolution{7}{}{

\stepheading{1. Read the spatial frequencies}
Low spatial frequencies describe smooth areas and gradual brightness changes.
High spatial frequencies describe sharp edges and fine details.

\stepheading{2. Read the graph}
A high surface means high gain; a low surface means low gain.

Near the center, $u=v=0$, the magnitude is close to $1$:
low spatial frequencies pass strongly.

Farther from the center, the magnitude falls toward $0$:
high spatial frequencies are suppressed.

\stepheading{3. Effect on an image}
Smooth image regions remain, while sharp edges and fine details become weaker.
The image becomes smoother or blurred.

\noteheading{Final answer}
\[
\boxed{\text{Low-pass filter}}
\]
It passes low spatial frequencies and attenuates high spatial frequencies,
giving smoothing / blurring.

\noteheading{Remember}
High center + low outside $\Rightarrow$ low-pass.
}

\SignalQuestion{9}{%
Given the two matrices representing the input $x(i,j)$ and output $y(i,j)$ of a
linear 2D filter: describe the $3\times3$ point spread function (PSF) or kernel
$h(i,j)$ that transforms $x(i,j)$ into $y(i,j)$.
Assume boundary conditions are zero, meaning pixels outside the matrix boundaries
of $x(i,j)$ and $y(i,j)$ are zero.
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=250pt]{assets/2025-01-07_Q09_input-matrix-original.png}\par
\vspace{5pt}
\includegraphics[width=250pt]{assets/2025-01-07_Q09_output-matrix-original.png}
\end{center}
\hfill(6p)
}

\SignalSolution{9}{}{%
\textit{Verified reference method, with the overlap calculations shown.}

\stepheading{1. Read one scaled impulse response}
An input pixel with value $A$ produces a copy of $A\,h$, centered at that pixel.
Overlapping copies add together.

Use $x(1,1)=112$. Its top output row is $(0,14,0)$;
no other input contributes to those three cells.
\[
\text{Top kernel row}
=\frac1{112}\begin{bmatrix}0&14&0\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac18&0\end{bmatrix}.
\]

\stepheading{2. Find the center and direct neighbors}
The center output is $56$, so
\[
\text{Center weight}=\frac{56}{112}=\frac12.
\]
The left, right and lower neighbor outputs are $14$.
The zero top-corner weights just found rule out diagonal overlaps at these cells.
\[
\text{Each direct-neighbor weight}
=\frac{14}{112}=\frac18.
\]

\stepheading{3. Remove the overlapping contributions}
The lower corners of the output patch also receive values from other input pixels.
Subtract those first, then divide by $112$.

At $y(2,0)=10$, the input value $80$ contributes $80/8=10$.
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Lower-left weight}
&=\frac{10-80/8}{112}\\
&=\frac{10-10}{112}\\
&=0.
\end{aligned}
\]
At $y(2,2)=36$, the inputs $224$ and $64$ contribute $224/8$ and $64/8$.
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Lower-right weight}
&=\frac{36-224/8-64/8}{112}\\
&=\frac{36-28-8}{112}\\
&=0.
\end{aligned}
\]

\stepheading{4. Check an overlapping output}
At $(3,3)$, three neighboring inputs contribute:
\[
\begin{aligned}
y(3,3)&=\frac{224}{8}+\frac{64}{8}+\frac{176}{8}\\
&=28+8+22\\
&=58.
\end{aligned}
\]
This agrees with the given output matrix.

\noteheading{Final answer}
\[
\boxed{h(i,j)=\begin{bmatrix}
0&\frac18&0\\[3pt]
\frac18&\frac12&\frac18\\[3pt]
0&\frac18&0
\end{bmatrix}}
\]
A cross-shaped averaging kernel: center weight $1/2$, four direct neighbors $1/8$,
and zero corners. The weights sum to $1$.

\noteheading{Remember}
An impulse gives a scaled kernel. Subtract overlapping copies, then divide by the input value.
}
