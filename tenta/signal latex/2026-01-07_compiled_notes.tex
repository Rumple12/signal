\SignalQuestion{1}{%
What do the following signal processing concepts mean? Be clear, concise, and relevant.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*),leftmargin=16pt,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt]
\item Convolution\hfill(1p)
\item Aliasing\hfill(1p)
\item LTI system\hfill(1p)
\item Spectrogram\hfill(1p)
\item Analogue signal\hfill(1p)
\item Reconstruction\hfill(1p)
\end{enumerate}
}

\SignalSolution{1}{a}{%
\noteheading{Final answer}
\textbf{Convolution} combines two signals by reversing one in time and shifting
it across the other. At each shift, multiply the overlapping values and add
the products (integrate for continuous-time signals).

In an LTI system, convolving the input with the impulse response gives the output.
}

\SignalSolution{1}{b}{%
\noteheading{Final answer}
\textbf{Aliasing} occurs when a signal is sampled too slowly, causing a high
frequency to appear as a lower frequency in the samples.

\textbf{From your notes:} with a sampling frequency of $30$ Hz, the folding limit is
\[
\frac{30}{2}=15\ \text{Hz}.
\]
The $10$ Hz component stays at $10$ Hz. The $25$ Hz component is above the
folding limit and appears at
\[
|25-30|=|-5|=5\ \text{Hz}.
\]
\textbf{Wording correction:} $15$ Hz is the folding limit; the sampling frequency
is $30$ Hz.
}

\SignalSolution{1}{c}{%
\noteheading{Final answer}
\textbf{LTI} means \textbf{linear} and \textbf{time-invariant}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Linear:} adding inputs or multiplying them by constants adds or
scales their outputs in the same way.
\item \textbf{Time-invariant:} delaying the input delays the output by the same
amount, without otherwise changing the output.
\end{itemize}
}

\SignalSolution{1}{d}{%
\noteheading{Final answer}
A \textbf{Spectrogram} shows how a signal's frequency content changes over time.
Time is on one axis, frequency on the other, and color or brightness shows
the strength of each frequency.

It is formed by analyzing successive short sections of the signal.
}

\SignalSolution{1}{e}{%
\noteheading{Final answer}
An \textbf{Analogue signal} is continuous in time and amplitude: it has a value
at every instant, and its amplitude can take any value within its range.
}

\SignalSolution{1}{f}{%
\noteheading{Final answer}
\textbf{Reconstruction} recreates a continuous-time signal from its samples.
If sampling was too slow, aliasing makes the reconstructed signal inaccurate.

\textbf{Wording correction:} the ``recreating continuous signals'' phrase in
your notes describes reconstruction; the note labels it as sampling.
}

\SignalSolution{2}{a}{%
We need to classify the system from the blocks and the feedback path.

\stepheading{1. Check causality}
Your notes check whether the output needs future input.
Both $T$ blocks are delays, so only present and past information is used.
There is no future input. The system is \textbf{causal}.

\stepheading{2. Check FIR or IIR}
The feedback loop returns the delayed signal to the summing point repeatedly.
An impulse can therefore keep producing nonzero outputs indefinitely.
The system is \textbf{IIR}.

\noteheading{Final answer}
The system is \textbf{causal and IIR}.

\SignalSolution{2}{b}{%
Find the difference equation by working backward from $y[n]$, as in your notes.

\stepheading{1. Undo the output gain}

\stepheading{2. Follow the feedback path}

\SignalQuestion{4}{%
The signal $x(t)=3\cos(50\pi t)+4\sin(20\pi t-0.1)$ is sampled and reconstructed
according to the figure below.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*),leftmargin=16pt,itemsep=5pt,topsep=3pt]
\item What is the lowest sampling frequency $f_s$ [Hz] to enable a full reconstruction
of the signal with the same frequency contents?\hfill(1p)
\item If the signal is sampled with $f_s=30$ Hz, reconstructed and filtered with an
ideal LP-filter with cut-off frequency $f_c=30$ Hz, what frequencies does the output
signal $\widetilde{x}_{LP}(t)$ then contain. [Hint: there is aliasing]\hfill(2p)
\item Draw the amplitude spectrum for the signal $x(t)$ and sketch (in the same diagram)
the frequency response for a real (non-ideal) antialiasing filter that allow full
reconstruction of the signal.\hfill(1p)
\end{enumerate}

\SignalSolution{4}{a}{%
We need the lowest sampling rate that preserves both frequencies.

\stepheading{1. Find the signal frequencies}
Use $\omega=2\pi f$, as in your notes:
\[
\begin{aligned}
f_1&=\frac{50\pi}{2\pi}=25\ \text{Hz},\\
f_2&=\frac{20\pi}{2\pi}=10\ \text{Hz}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The highest signal frequency is $25$ Hz.

\stepheading{2. Double the highest frequency}
\[
f_{s,\min}=2\cdot25=50\ \text{Hz}.
\]

\noteheading{Final answer}
The \textbf{Nyquist limit} is $\boxed{50\ \text{Hz}}$, as in your notes.
A real filter needs a sampling rate above this limit to allow for its roll-off.
}

\SignalSolution{4}{b}{%
Find the reconstructed frequencies after sampling at $30$ Hz.
\textbf{Reconstruction assumption:} use the low-frequency (baseband) reconstruction assumed in your notes.

\stepheading{1. Check the sampling rate}
$30$ Hz is below the $50$ Hz Nyquist limit, so aliasing occurs.

\stepheading{2. Find the folding limit}
\[
\frac{f_s}{2}=\frac{30}{2}=15\ \text{Hz}.
\]

\stepheading{3. Check each component}
The $10$ Hz component is below $15$ Hz, so it stays at $10$ Hz.
The $25$ Hz component is above $15$ Hz and folds to
\[
|25-30|=|-5|=5\ \text{Hz}.
\]
Both $5$ Hz and $10$ Hz are below the LP-filter cutoff of $30$ Hz and pass through.

\noteheading{Final answer}
\[
\boxed{5\ \text{Hz}\quad\text{and}\quad10\ \text{Hz}.}
\]
\textbf{Note correction:} $15$ Hz is the folding limit. The $25$ Hz component
is aliased during sampling, rather than simply cut off by the LP filter.
}

\SignalSolution{4}{c}{%
Draw the spectrum and filter shape together, as in your notes.
Use your \textbf{one-sided} amplitude spectrum:
\begin{itemize}
\item $4$ at $10$ Hz from the sine.
\item $3$ at $25$ Hz from the cosine.
\end{itemize}
A \textbf{low-pass} anti-aliasing filter keeps these components and attenuates
higher frequencies \textbf{before sampling}.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*),leftmargin=16pt,itemsep=5pt,topsep=3pt]

\item After exposure, a digital camera applies a sequence of signal processing operations

\end{enumerate}

\SignalSolution{6}{a}{%
We follow the light until each pixel has stored RGB numbers.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=16pt,itemsep=7pt,topsep=5pt]
\item \textbf{Illumination and reflection.}
A source emits continuous light. Objects reflect different amounts and wavelengths
of that light, carrying information about the scene and its colors.

\item \textbf{Optical projection.}
The lens focuses the reflected light onto the sensor, forming a continuous
two-dimensional optical image.

\item \textbf{Sampling and sensing.}
A grid of sensor elements samples the image at separate pixel positions.
During exposure, collected light becomes electrical charge or voltage.
In a typical Bayer sensor, color filters let each element measure one red,
green or blue component.

\item \textbf{Quantization.}
An analog-to-digital converter assigns each sensor measurement a discrete
integer value. This makes the measured intensities digital.

\item \textbf{RGB reconstruction.}
Where color components are missing, demosaicing estimates them from neighboring
measurements to obtain RGB values for each pixel.

\item \textbf{Storage.}
After the required camera processing, the digital RGB values are stored in memory.
\end{enumerate}

\noteheading{Final answer}
The stored image is a grid of pixels, each with digital R, G and B values.
}

\SignalSolution{6}{b}{%
Name three operations and state what each does.

\noteheading{Final answer}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=16pt,itemsep=8pt,topsep=5pt]
\item \textbf{White balance:}
adjusts the color-channel gains to compensate for the light source's color,
so neutral objects appear neutral.

\item \textbf{Noise reduction:}
reduces unwanted random variations caused by the sensor and electronics,
making the image less noisy.

\item \textbf{Sharpening:}
increases edge contrast so outlines and fine detail appear clearer.
\end{enumerate}

\SignalQuestion{7}{%
Linear image operators can be analyzed by studying how they act on elementary image
components and how these responses combine to form the output image.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\alph*),leftmargin=16pt,itemsep=5pt,topsep=3pt]
\item Starting from the definition of a linear image operator acting on a point source
\[
O\{\delta(x-\alpha,y-\beta)\}=h(x,y,\alpha,\beta)
\]
explain why a general image can be interpreted as a weighted sum of point sources,
and how this leads to the operator expression for the output image $g(\alpha,\beta)$.
\par\nobreak\noindent\makebox[\linewidth][r]{(3p)}\par
\item Assume the PSF is shift-invariant. Derive the simplified expression for the output
image $g(\alpha,\beta)$ and explain why this can be implemented using convolution.
\par\nobreak\noindent\makebox[\linewidth][r]{(3p)}\par
\end{enumerate}
}

\SignalSolution{7}{a}{%
\textit{Newly authored: no matching handwritten solution found.}
We need the output image from the responses to individual points.

\stepheading{1. Write the input as weighted point sources}
Let $f(x,y)$ be the input image. The symbol $\delta$ represents a unit point source.
Use $(\xi,\eta)$ for the position of each source, so the coordinate names do not clash.
\[
f(x,y)=\iint_{\mathbb R^2} f(\xi,\eta)\,
\delta(x-\xi,y-\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta.
\]
The integral adds contributions over the whole image. A point at $(\xi,\eta)$
has weight $f(\xi,\eta)$, its image intensity. The delta selects the contribution
at $(x,y)$, so adding all these weighted points gives back $f(x,y)$.

\stepheading{2. Apply the linear operator}
Linearity means that the response to a weighted sum is the same weighted sum
of the individual responses. Therefore,
\[
g(x,y)=O\{f(x,y)\}
\]
becomes
\[
g(x,y)=\iint_{\mathbb R^2} f(\xi,\eta)\,
O\{\delta(x-\xi,y-\eta)\}\,d\xi\,d\eta.
\]
From the given definition, the response to this point source is
\[
O\{\delta(x-\xi,y-\eta)\}=h(x,y,\xi,\eta).
\]
Here $h$ is the \textbf{point spread function (PSF)}: the output produced by one
unit point source. Its first coordinate pair is the output position; the second
is the source position.

\stepheading{3. Substitute the point-source response}
Replace the operator acting on each delta by $h$. Use $(\alpha,\beta)$ for
the output position, as requested in the question; $(\xi,\eta)$ still labels the sources.

\noteheading{Final answer}
\[
\boxed{g(\alpha,\beta)=\iint_{\mathbb R^2} f(\xi,\eta)\,
h(\alpha,\beta,\xi,\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta.}
\]
The output is the sum of all point-source responses, each weighted by the input
intensity at that source.
}

\SignalSolution{7}{b}{%
\textit{Newly authored: no matching handwritten solution found.}
We need to simplify the output when the PSF is shift-invariant.
Let $f$ be the input image and $h$ the point spread function, the response to
a unit point source. Here $(\alpha,\beta)$ is the output position and
$(\xi,\eta)$ is a source position.

\stepheading{1. Use shift invariance}
Shift invariance means that moving a point source moves its response by the
same amount, without changing its shape. The response therefore depends only
on the distance and direction from source to output:
\[
h(\alpha,\beta,\xi,\eta)=h(\alpha-\xi,\beta-\eta).
\]
On the right, $h$ is the PSF for a source at the origin, shifted to $(\xi,\eta)$.

\stepheading{2. Substitute into the result from (a)}
Before the substitution, the output is
\[
g(\alpha,\beta)=\iint_{\mathbb R^2} f(\xi,\eta)\,
h(\alpha,\beta,\xi,\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta.
\]
Replace the four-coordinate PSF by the shifted, two-coordinate PSF:
\[
g(\alpha,\beta)=\iint_{\mathbb R^2} f(\xi,\eta)\,
h(\alpha-\xi,\beta-\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta.
\]
We now use the same PSF shape for every source position.

\stepheading{3. Recognize two-dimensional convolution}
This integral is the definition of two-dimensional convolution. The symbol $*$
denotes convolution.

\noteheading{Final answer}
\[
\boxed{g(\alpha,\beta)=(f*h)(\alpha,\beta).}
\]
For each output position, flip the PSF in both coordinates, shift it to that
position, multiply it by the input image, and add the products. For digital
images, this addition is a sum over pixels instead of an integral.
The same PSF kernel is used throughout the image, so convolution implements
the shift-invariant operator.
}

\SignalSolution{8}{}{%
\textit{Based on your August 2026 handwritten notes, reused with your permission.}
We need to connect the original image, its histogram and cumulative histogram
to the enhanced image.

\stepheading{1. Read the original histogram (c): $h$}
The horizontal axis is pixel intensity: dark on the left, bright on the right.
The vertical axis counts how many pixels have each intensity.
The original image (a) has relatively few dark pixels and many middle-to-bright
pixels. This uneven distribution helps explain its weak contrast.

\stepheading{2. Read the original cumulative histogram (e): $H$}
Starting from the darkest level, add the pixel counts up to each intensity.
Express this as a fraction of all pixels. Then $H(r)$ tells us the fraction
of pixels with intensity at or below $r$.
Where many pixels have similar intensities, the curve rises steeply; where
there are few pixels, it rises slowly. These changes give (e) its curved shape.

\stepheading{3. Use $H$ to change each pixel value}
Histogram equalization redistributes the intensities to improve contrast.
Keep the transformation written in your notes:
\[
\boxed{s=(L-1)H(r).}
\]
Here $r$ is the original pixel value, $s$ is the enhanced value, and $L$ is
the number of possible gray levels. Thus $L-1$ is the highest output value.
For each pixel:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=16pt,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
\item Read its original intensity $r$.
\item Find $H(r)$ on curve (e), a fraction between $0$ and $1$.
\item Multiply by $L-1$ to obtain $s$, and replace $r$ by $s$.
\end{enumerate}
The fraction is converted into an output gray level. Apply this same rule
to every pixel to produce image (b).

\stepheading{4. Read the enhanced cumulative histogram (f): $H'$}
This is the cumulative histogram after the transformation. A straight line
is the ideal goal: the intensities are distributed evenly across the gray levels.
Real digital results may have small steps, as your notes show.
\textbf{Note correction:} ``constant intensity'' in your notes should mean
an even intensity distribution, not that every pixel has the same value.

\stepheading{5. Read the enhanced histogram (d): $h'$}
This counts pixels at each new intensity in image (b). Dark and bright levels
are better represented, so the distribution is more balanced and contrast improves.
It need not be perfectly flat; the plotted histogram still has peaks and gaps.

\noteheading{Final answer}
Use the original histogram (c) to form the cumulative histogram (e).
Apply $s=(L-1)H(r)$ to each pixel of (a). This gives enhanced image (b),
with histogram (d) and the more nearly straight cumulative histogram (f).
